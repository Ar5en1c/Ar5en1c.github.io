Where bias hides in an AI product, and how to catch it

Most writing about AI bias is aimed at researchers. This page is for the people who actually ship the product: a founder, a small team, someone who tests on the laptop they have. I started from my AIML-500 discussion post on human bias and turned it into three things you can try, using examples from my own work and from published studies.

00

The idea in one minute

AI learns from data that people choose, label, test and read. Our course lists those as five places bias gets in: data selection, labeling, algorithm design, testing, and the way results are interpreted. I can't show you all five with a slider, but I've hit three of them in my own product or seen them clearly in published studies, so those are the three below. Each one is something you can try before you read the explanation.

01

Your average can hide most of your users

This is interpretation, the last stage. Below are 20 made-up users and how many job applications each one sent. Nineteen of them are fixed. Drag the slider to change how many the heaviest user sent, and watch what the average does while the median barely moves.

This happened to me for real in June 2026. My dashboard said 8.3 applications per active user, but the median was 2, because one user with 399 applications made up 52.7% of the total. I had been tracking a number that described one person. Now I report the median next to the average and look at groups separately, so a heavy user or the biggest group can't speak for everyone.

02

You only test on who you are

This is testing. In June 2026 I moved the AI in my Chrome extension into the user's browser. About 39% of my users were on Windows, and I built it without a Windows machine. Many Windows graphics cards couldn't run the model, and I found out when friends told me. Try a different release plan: send the update to a small group first, as long as that group looks like your real users.

The same blind spot shows up at a much larger scale. Buolamwini and Gebru (2018) found that common face-analysis benchmarks were mostly lighter-skinned faces. When they tested three commercial gender classifiers on a balanced set, the systems misclassified darker-skinned women up to 34.7% of the time, against at most 0.8% for lighter-skinned men.

Each is the highest rate among the three systems for that group.

My Windows miss left some users with a model that wouldn't run. Theirs costs people real consequences. The cause is the same: the people building and testing the system didn't look like everyone who would use it.

03

A label can quietly measure the wrong thing

This is labeling, and I think it's the sneakiest one. Obermeyer and colleagues (2019) studied a health-care algorithm used to decide which patients get extra help. It predicted each patient's future cost, as a stand-in for how sick they were. But less money had been spent on Black patients with the same needs, so at the same risk score, Black patients were considerably sicker. Switch the label below and see what changes.

Black patients flagged for extra help
17.7%

The authors' estimate was that the change would raise that share from 17.7% to 46.5%. Cost looked like a reasonable stand-in, and it even looked accurate by some measures, so the problem was easy to miss. Before a model learns from any signal, such as spending, clicks or past approvals, I now write down what the signal really stands in for and who it might misrepresent.

04

A checklist you can take to your team

These are the five places from the course, each with one question to ask in a team meeting and the example from this page, where there is one.

Where it gets inOne question to askSeen here
Data selectionWho is missing from the data we chose?Face benchmarks that were mostly lighter-skinned faces
LabelingWhat does this label really measure?Health cost standing in for health needs
Algorithm designWhat did we assume would be fair, and who agreed?Not shown on this page
TestingWhich users can't we reproduce ourselves?No Windows machine, 39% of users on Windows
InterpretationDoes this number describe most people, or one?Average of 8.3, median of 2

The hardest part is getting a team to ask these questions without feeling accused. A "bias audit" can sound like blame, so I would call it a quality check, start with one question on one release, share my own misses first, and give someone the job of disagreeing with me.

05

How I adapted it for this audience

  • The class post was written for classmates and a professor. This page is for founders and small teams, so it starts with a dashboard number everyone has seen instead of with definitions.
  • I replaced paragraphs of explanation with things to try. Each of the three sections lets the reader move something before reading the reasoning.
  • Every number is either from my own product or from a named study. The user chart is labeled as illustrative so no one mistakes it for data.
  • One example is from my own mistakes, on purpose. People take a lesson from "I got this wrong" more easily than from a warning.
06

What I took from it

  • Bias comes from several small choices that each looked reasonable at the time, so I'd treat the fix as a habit of asking questions instead of one audit.
  • My own examples were milder than the published ones, and I think that helps. Seeing the same pattern in a small product makes the big cases easier to believe.
  • The pilot model on this page is simple. It assumes the small group looks like all users and that someone reports the problem. Real teams would have to check both.
  • Next I'd show this to someone who isn't in tech and see which section they skip, which is the same feedback step I used for artifact 3.
